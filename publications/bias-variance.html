Bias vs. Variance in RL

It's been a while since I posted this tweet. It's about how, as RL progressed, it relied more on variance instead of bias. I call this idea “it's better to have no beliefs than wrong beliefs.”

Bias vs. Variance in RL:

As we're training models with longer and longer-horizon trajectories, a value function or similar bias mechanism seems inevitable. Historically, as RL progressed, it added more variance and paid for that with more compute.

Dynamic programming → model-based → value-based → policy gradient → PPO → GRPO.

Even GRPO removed the learned value function, replacing it by computing the advantage purely from the group mean. It has more variance than PPO but one fewer learned component making assumptions about value structure. In a language-modeling setting, we saw that compute is growing so quickly that it is actually cheap to sample many responses per prompt compared with learning a value function.

The historical winner has been the high-variance, low-bias side because of the available scale of compute.

A high-variance method works when you can learn the signal by paying for lots of compute at scale. When the signal itself is too rare to observe even at scale, you need bias to guide the search toward the signal in the first place.

In a long-horizon setting where the signal is sparse, even with enormous compute, we cannot learn it. Variance cannot be averaged out by compute alone. We need some form of inductive bias to obtain a signal for the gradient.

Methods that add just enough bias to find the signal and let variance average out at scale are what I find optimal.

High-bias methods make strong assumptions to reduce noise. They're efficient when those assumptions hold; in that case, you need less data to get a stable signal. But if the assumptions are wrong, incomplete, or do not hold at scale, then the ceiling is the assumption itself.

High-variance methods make weaker assumptions. They're noisy and need more data, but they are not constrained by wrong beliefs about the world. Scale gives them more samples, which averages out the variance. The ceiling is just compute and data.

When compute was scarce, bias was acceptable. You could not afford to average out variance anyway, so you let the assumptions do the work. When compute became abundant, the assumptions became the bottleneck. High-variance methods suddenly got a chance to prove themselves.

The idea that the ceiling is just compute and data is becoming cheaper to fulfill with on-policy algorithms. We know that compute has scaled over the years, beating even Moore's law, which means that doing more rollouts is becoming possible and actually works. This is why we can afford to remove bias. One example is GRPO, which removes the learned value function entirely.

Variance is a statistical problem. More data solves it, and the solution scales with compute. Bias is a structural problem. Wrong assumptions are instantiated in the method, and data cannot fix them: “you just get a more precise answer to the wrong question.”

However, there is one small caveat: high-variance methods win when the signal exists and is observable at the scale you can afford. When the signal itself is too rare to observe even at scale, you need bias to guide the search toward the signal in the first place. But it needs to be the right kind of inductive bias: one that guides the search toward the signal so that variance can later average out at scale.

The idea is “scale is a solution that doesn't require being right about anything. Bias requires being right.” When we design an architecture, we are betting that a structure exists in the problem. If it does, then we have compressed the search space, and we need less data. If it does not, we have built a wall. This resonates with The Bitter Lesson by Sutton et al.

Why don't high-bias, off-policy methods scale well?

I learned about this research, Q-learning Is Not Yet Scalable (thanks to Lucas!), and my rabbit hole started again because it empirically supports the insights shared in the tweet above.

This research essentially explains why off-policy methods have not scaled as well as on-policy methods.

Excerpt from Seohong Park's research post:

What's the problem?

Then, what fundamentally makes Q-learning not readily scalable to complex, long-horizon problems, unlike other objectives? Here is my answer:

$$ \mathbb{E}_{(s,a,r,s') \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[ \left( Q_\theta(s,a) - \underbrace{ \left(r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q_{\bar{\theta}}(s',a')\right) }_{\text{Biased }\left(\text{i.e., } \neq Q^*(s,a)\right)} \right)^2 \right] $$

Q-learning struggles to scale because the prediction targets are biased, and these biases accumulate over the horizon. The presence of bias accumulation is a fundamental limitation that is unique to Q-learning (TD learning). For example, there are no biases in prediction targets in other scalable objectives (e.g., next-token prediction, denoising diffusion, contrastive learning, etc.) or at least these biases do not accumulate over the horizon (e.g., BYOL, DINO, etc.).

This points to the same problem we've been considering: Q-learning struggles to scale because its prediction targets are biased, and these biases accumulate over the horizon. This accumulation is a fundamental limitation unique to Q-learning (TD learning).

This happens in Q-learning because “we're using a guess to improve a guess”:

$$ Q_{\text{target}} = r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q_{\bar{\theta}}(s', a') $$

The target itself uses \(Q_{\bar{\theta}}\) to approximate Q-function. It was itself built from another approximate Q-function, which was built from another. This is the bias that compounds rather than smoothing out.

With longer and longer horizons, the biases in bootstrapped targets become difficult to mitigate with more data and a larger model. In the research, they prepared a set of tasks that require complex policy learning. The thing is, none of the standard, widely used offline RL algorithms were able to solve all of these tasks, even with 1B-sized datasets, which are 1,000 times larger than typical datasets used in offline RL. More importantly, their performance often plateaued far below optimal performance.

We know that GAE smooths out this bias with more data, but why does it compound here? The target in supervised learning comes from outside the model, while Q-learning's target contains its own Q-function. This propagates errors to subsequent steps instead of averaging them out. Supervised learning carries ground truth that is independent of the model's prediction. In Q-learning, the “ground truth” used for training at each step is contaminated by the current prediction because we are using a guess to improve a guess. Therefore, more data means more contaminated training signal, not more correction.
